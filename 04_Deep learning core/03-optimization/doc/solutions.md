# Solutions — 03 Optimization

1. **Momentum** was introduced because plain SGD oscillates back and forth in narrow ravines instead of moving efficiently toward the minimum. **RMSProp** was introduced because AdaGrad's ever-growing sum of squared gradients causes its effective learning rate to shrink to zero and halt learning prematurely. **AdamW** was introduced because Adam's naive weight decay gets incorrectly scaled by the adaptive `√v̂` term, coupling regularization strength to gradient magnitude in a way that hurts generalization.

2. `EMA₁ = 0.9(0) + 0.1(10) = 1.0`. `EMA₂ = 0.9(1.0) + 0.1(0) = 0.9`. `EMA₃ = 0.9(0.9) + 0.1(10) = 1.81`. Notice the EMA never jumps as violently as the raw values do (which swing 10 → 0 → 10) — it lags behind and smooths the signal, weighting recent observations more but never fully "forgetting" the past, which is exactly the behavior that makes gradient/velocity tracking stable.

3. With a steeper ravine (`50*y**2` instead of `10*y**2`), plain SGD's oscillation typically gets noticeably worse (larger, more violent zig-zags, and it may even diverge depending on the learning rate) because the gradient in the `y` direction becomes much larger, causing bigger overshooting steps. Momentum, RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW should all still handle it — that's precisely the point of their per-parameter/direction-aware adjustments — though Momentum alone may need a smaller learning rate to stay fully stable at this steepness.

4. AdaGrad's trajectory should visibly slow down and eventually nearly stop moving well before reaching `(0,0)`, because `G` (the running *sum* of squared gradients) keeps growing every step, forever shrinking the effective learning rate `η/√G` toward zero — even though the true gradient is still nonzero and the minimum hasn't been reached. This exact failure mode — the optimizer prematurely giving up — is the specific problem RMSProp was designed to fix by using an EMA (which "forgets" old gradients) instead of an ever-growing sum.

5. They typically do **not** converge to exactly the same point (or take a different path to a similar point) — with naive L2-via-gradient, the effective decay strength on `w` gets divided by `√v̂`, meaning parameters with large historical gradients (large `v̂`) get *weaker* regularization than intended, while AdamW applies the same `weight_decay` shrinkage uniformly regardless of `v̂`. This mismatch is the exact bug AdamW's paper identified — regularization strength should not depend on the adaptive learning-rate term.

6. At `lr=0.1`: plain SGD and Momentum-based methods often still make reasonable (if slower) progress, since `0.1` isn't wildly outside their typical operating range. RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW, however, typically converge *much* more slowly at `lr=0.1` than at their tuned defaults (`0.3`/`0.5`) — because their update magnitude is already divided by `√v̂` (roughly normalizing the step size), so their "effective" learning rate at a given `lr` differs meaningfully from plain SGD's, and adaptive optimizers are often used with comparatively higher nominal learning rates than plain SGD for this reason.
