# Solutions — 10 Modalities, Regulation & Ethics

1. (a) Attention Visualization or LIME for Text (highlights influential words); (b) Grad-CAM or Saliency Maps (highlights influential image regions); (c) Temporal SHAP or Pattern Attributions (highlights influential time steps/subsequences).

2. GDPR Article 22 requirements: (1) explanation of system functionality, (2) the weight of each deciding factor, (3) reasons for the specific outcome, (4) clear instructions on how to challenge the decision. For a loan bot: (1) "This system evaluates income, debt, and credit history to predict repayment likelihood"; (2) "Debt-to-income ratio contributed 45% of the decision weight"; (3) "You were denied because your debt-to-income ratio exceeds our threshold"; (4) "You may request a human review by contacting [X] within 30 days."

3. Tier 1 restricts to glass-box models because in high-risk medical contexts, the FDA is prioritizing **verifiability and accountability over raw accuracy** — a clinician needs to be able to fully trace and validate the model's reasoning before trusting a diagnosis that could affect patient safety, since even a small, hard-to-detect error in a black-box model's logic could cause serious harm, and post-hoc explanations (however good) are still approximations, not the model's literal reasoning.

4. Structurally, both methods work by identifying which *input units* (pixels for images, tokens/words for text) the model weighted most heavily when producing its output, usually via gradients or learned attention weights, and rendering that as a heatmap or highlight overlay. They differ in the granularity and semantics of the "unit": Grad-CAM highlights spatial regions of an image (continuous, 2D), while Attention Visualization highlights discrete tokens in a sequence (1D, symbolic).

5. Example: none of GDPR/FDA/Algorithmic Accountability Act cleanly covers a social media feed-ranking algorithm — it's not "automated decision-making" about an individual in the strict legal sense GDPR targets, it's not medical, and current Algorithmic Accountability Act proposals mostly target formal high-stakes decisions (hiring, lending, housing). Arguably such systems should still owe users some explanation of why certain content is ranked/suppressed, given their outsized influence on information access — an open regulatory gap the theory's "Open Research Questions" section (see `11-limitations-and-open-problems`) implicitly points toward.
